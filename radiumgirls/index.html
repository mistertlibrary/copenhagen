The Radium Girls


Or,
What’s Glowing On with Watch Dials from Orange?

Briefing

In 1917, a company in Orange, New Jersey, began painting glowing numbers on watch dials. As many as 300 workers, mostly young women, did the painting. Supervisors specifically instructed them to point their brushes with their lips (you know, to ensure precision and make sure the numbers looked nice).

The company’s own records survive at the National Archives. They were so radioactive the original papers were destroyed for safety purposes.

Today, you’ll work through five exhibits, solve a few chemistry problems, and decide which sources you can trust. Each code unlocks the next exhibit; log every source as you go.

Exhibit A

1917

The Paint

The company sold its glowing paint as “Undark.” One archive describes it as radium mixed with zinc sulfide. A state report describes it as glue, water, and radium powder.

Exhibit B

1924

The Report

The company hired Harvard scientists to inspect the plant. They found radium dust everywhere and linked it to the workers’ illnesses. The company’s president threatened to sue if they published their findings. A different, rosier report actually reached the New Jersey Department of Labor.

Exhibit C

1925

The Examiner

Dr. Harrison Martland, the Essex County physician, began studying the dead and dying painters. He concluded that tiny amounts of radium in the paint had killed them. Radium in the body keeps decaying, and one of its products is a gas you can breathe out (and in, for that matter; test your basement and get a fan if you need one!).

Exhibit D

1928

The Settlement

Five former painters sued. The company settled before trial: $10,000 each, $600 a year for life (but how long a life?), and medical costs.

But when? One government source says the women accepted on June 4, 1928. The National Archives dates the signed agreement June 8, 1928. An encyclopedia says autumn.

Exhibit E

Today

The Cleanup

The plant closed in 1926. Decades later, it became a federal Superfund site, and the cleanup didn’t finish until 2006. (Today, it’s a park; you can go visit!) The company’s papers turned up at another Superfund site in Pennsylvania, still radioactive.

Hint

100 years is 100/1,600 of a half-life. The fraction remaining is 1/2 raised to that power.

Debrief

Debrief

The women lost their health and most lost their lives. Their case helped make workplace illness a corporate liability.
